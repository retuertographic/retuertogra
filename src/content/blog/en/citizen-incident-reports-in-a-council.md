A street light that won't come on, a pothole in the road, an overflowing bin or a branch about to fall. Every day residents spot problems in their area and want to report them. The challenge for a council, a residential community or a residents' association isn't getting reports in, but getting them in properly: with enough information to act on and a way of knowing where each one stands.

## Why the usual system falls short

In many towns, incidents come in through very different channels: a call to the switchboard, an email, a comment on social media or a chat in the street with a councillor. The result tends to be the same:

- **The exact location is missing.** "On the street by the church" could be anywhere along it.
- **There are no photos**, so the team has to travel just to assess the problem.
- **The same problem is reported several times** by different people.
- **Nobody knows the status.** The resident gets no reply and calls again, or stops reporting.

## The four stages of a well-managed incident

1. **Reporting.** The resident describes the problem, attaches photos or a video and marks the spot. [[Geolocation|geolocalizacion]] avoids vague addresses.
2. **Classification.** The incident is assigned to a category (street lighting, roads, cleaning, parks…) and to a responsible person or department.
3. **Action.** The responsible team updates the status and records what has been done.
4. **Closure and notification.** Once resolved, the person who reported it is notified. That closure is what builds trust.

## What to prepare before launching an app

- **A short list of categories** that citizens understand at first glance.
- **Clear owners** for each category, with cover during holidays.
- **A few statuses** (received, under review, in progress, resolved) whose meaning the whole team shares.
- **A privacy notice.** Reports may include personal data and photos, and must be handled in line with [[GDPR|rgpd]].
- **A communication plan**: posters in council buildings, the website, social media and, why not, a [[QR code|codigo-qr]] on the noticeboard that leads straight to the app.

## What each side gains

Citizens can report faster and know what happens to their report. The council team receives complete, organised information on a management dashboard, without copying data from one channel to another. And the organisation gets an action history that shows where problems cluster, which helps with planning. Good [[user experience|ux]] is essential: if reporting an incident takes more than a minute, many people will keep phoning.

## How to measure whether it works

Look at the average resolution time per category, the share of incidents that arrive with a photo and location, and the number of reports still coming in by phone. Those three [[KPIs|kpi]] show whether the new channel is being used and whether it speeds up the work.
