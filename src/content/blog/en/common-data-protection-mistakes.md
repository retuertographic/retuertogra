Most data protection problems in a small business don't come from major attacks, but from everyday habits: an email sent to every customer with all the addresses visible, a form with no information or a shared folder anyone can open. They're easy to fix once spotted. These are the most common ones.

## On your website

- **Forms with no information about processing.** Every contact or booking form should state, at least briefly, who processes the data and why, with a link to the privacy policy.
- **Pre-ticked consent boxes.** Consent has to be a clear action by the person. A pre-ticked box doesn't count.
- **Cookies without consent.** Cookies that aren't technical or strictly necessary, such as analytics or advertising cookies, require the user's prior consent. The banner should make rejecting them as easy as accepting them.
- **Legal notices copied from another website.** Your privacy policy has to describe what your business does, not what someone else's does.
- **A website without an [[SSL|ssl]] certificate.** If data is sent through a form, the connection must be encrypted.

## In email and messaging

- **Sending bulk emails with every recipient visible.** Use BCC or, better still, an [[email marketing|email-marketing]] tool.
- **Sending advertising without permission.** Commercial emails generally require prior consent, with exceptions such as existing customers and similar products. There must always be an easy way to unsubscribe.
- **Creating WhatsApp groups with customers.** Everyone can see everyone else's number. For customer messaging, [[WhatsApp Business|whatsapp-business]] with one-to-one chats or broadcast lists is preferable.

## Inside the business

- **Shared passwords or passwords on a sticky note.** Each person should have their own login, with the permissions they need.
- **Suppliers without a contract.** If your accountant, [[CRM|crm]] or hosting provider accesses your customers' data, you need a processor agreement with each of them.
- **Keeping data forever.** Set retention periods and delete what you no longer need.
- **Not knowing what to do when someone makes a request.** If a customer asks to access their data or have it deleted, you generally have to reply within one month. A simple procedure saves you improvising.
- **Out-of-date documentation.** Opening an online shop or switching tools changes your processing; your documents must reflect that.

## A quick five-step review

1. Go through every form on your website and its information notice.
2. Check the cookie banner: can visitors reject as easily as they accept?
3. List the suppliers that access data and check the contracts.
4. Review who has access to what and change any shared passwords.
5. Put a date in the diary for the next review of your documentation.

*This article is for general guidance only and is not a substitute for legal advice on specific cases.*
